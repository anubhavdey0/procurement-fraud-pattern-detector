A lead is a reason to open the tender files. It is not a finding.

This page lists groups of bidders whose pattern of bidding is unusual under a stated statistical test. Unusual patterns have ordinary causes, and each lead lists the ones to rule out first. Nothing here says that any firm, person or official broke a rule.

Firms, tenders and buyers appear under labels such as FIRM-0042. The key that maps labels back to the public records stays with the analyst who ran the screen.

What this data can and cannot show

Of 43,669 tenders in the source, 9,994 list who bid and 5,258 of those name a winner. 4,054 have both an award value and a buyer's estimate.

Losing bid amounts are not published. The screens with the best track record need them, so they cannot run here. Everything below rests on three weaker facts: who bid alongside whom, who won, and what the award was worth against the estimate. The checks at the end of this page show how much weaker.

  • Losing bid amounts are not published, so price screens cannot run.
  • Bidders are matched by normalised name; namesakes are merged and renamed firms are split.

Award prices against the estimate

4,012 awards have a usable ratio of award value to the buyer's estimate. The median is 0.950. The distribution is not smooth: 26% of awards sit at 0.90 of the estimate and 32% of awards sit at 1.00 of the estimate (to the nearest 0.01). Piles like these usually come from rules, such as a floor below which a bid is treated as abnormally low, or bidding in whole percentages of the estimate. They are a feature of the market, so the price screen compares each group with other tenders from the same buyers that drew as many bidders, and never reads the level on its own.

How far to trust these screens

Each screen was run on six sets of tenders in which a competition authority or a court identified a cartel. The number is the AUC: the chance that a cartel tender ranks above a competitive one. 0.5 is a coin toss. Below 0.5 the screen points the wrong way.

The variance screen is the most dependable of the seven. It separates cartel tenders (AUC of 0.6 or more) in 4 of 6 cases and fails in the rest. Screens that need bid amounts cannot run at all on a source that publishes only awards.

Method

Firms and tenders form a bipartite graph: a bid is a link between a firm and a tender. Two firms are linked when they bid on at least 3 tenders together and that is more than independent bidding would give (false discovery rate 0.01). Groups are grown on those links. Four screens are then applied to each group.

ScreenWhat it asksFires when
Closed group Is the group's bidding mostly with itself? After Wachs and Kertesz (2019). Exclusivity of 0.25 or more, and higher than in 20 reshuffled markets (p at most 0.05).
Winner rotation Do members take turns winning? A runs test on the sequence of winners, with a mid-p. More changes of winner than chance, at a false discovery rate of 0.10. Needs 8 wins.
Persistent losing bidder Does a member keep bidding and keep losing? Wins against equal odds. A member with 10 or more bids wins a quarter or less of the expected number, at a false discovery rate of 0.10.
Awards near the estimate Are the group's awards closer to the estimate than tenders from the same buyers that drew as many bidders? Stratified rank test (van Elteren), at a false discovery rate of 0.10. Needs 5 compared awards.

Every threshold is a judgement call and can be changed. Bidders are matched by name, so two people who share a name are merged and a renamed firm is split.
